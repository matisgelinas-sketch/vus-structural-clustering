Structural Clustering of Cancer-Risk Gene Variants

A hypothesis-generation approach using AlphaFold structures and machine learning to prioritize variants of uncertain significance
Matis Gelinas
August 30, 2026
01 — Motivation

The unclassified-variant problem

Genetic testing routinely finds “Variants of Uncertain Significance” (VUS) — changes that are neither clearly harmless nor clearly disease-causing
Classifying them (ACMG/AMP guidelines) requires converging clinical or experimental evidence
Computational or structural evidence sits at the weakest evidentiary tier — it can suggest leads, never confirm them
Goal: generate leads worth investigating further — not diagnose.
02 — Method

A two-phase approach

PHASE 1
Structural flagging
Flag VUS that sit close to a known disease-causing residue in 3D space — but far apart in the raw sequence, where a naive lookup would miss them
PHASE 2
Cross-gene validation
Pool the structural signal across genes and test with machine learning whether it actually predicts pathogenicity — on genes the model has never seen

04 — Phase 1

Flagging by structure, not sequence

The rule
Flag a VUS if it is within 6Å of a known pathogenic residue in 3D — by either backbone or side-chain atom position — but more than 10 positions apart in sequence
The sequence-distance requirement matters: without it, most flagged cases turn out to just be immediate neighbors in the chain — not a meaningful structural signal. Checking both atom types matters too: a residue's side chain, not its backbone, is often what's actually close to a pathogenic residue.
05 — Phase 2

What the model actually checks

For every variant, the model computes nine simple numbers — it never looks at the raw sequence or the raw structure directly.
One variant
Nine measurements
Model
Probability
The nine measurements
Backbone (Cα) and side-chain (Cβ) 3D distance to nearest known pathogenic residue
Sequence distance to that same residue, for each atom type
The ratio and the difference between sequence and 3D distance
Number of pathogenic residues nearby
Structural confidence at that position (pLDDT)
Whether the region is structurally disordered
Training
Shown thousands of variants already labeled Pathogenic or Benign, it learns which combinations of those nine numbers tend to go with each label
Checking a new variant
Computes the same nine numbers, then outputs a probability: how closely does this variant’s profile resemble known Pathogenic cases?
Logistic regression and a small random forest — simple, interpretable models, not a black box. We can see exactly which measurements matter.

07 — Key finding

Ruling out a confound

A natural worry: maybe the model was just learning “this is a well-structured, important part of the protein” — not genuine spatial clustering.
We tested this directly: removed structural-confidence as a factor and re-ran the analysis.

09 — Honest caveats

Limitations

Small dataset
~48,000 variants vs. ~71 million for genome-scale tools like AlphaMissense
Uneven performance
Accuracy varies a great deal from gene to gene — not a single reliable number
Static structures
No protein flexibility, complexes, or bound states modeled — side-chain (Cβ) position is a fixed proxy, not true rotamer flexibility
Not diagnostic
A hypothesis-generation tool throughout — every output is a candidate for further investigation, never a classification
10 — Next steps

Where this goes next

In progress now
Manually following up on a handful of the flagged candidate variants — cross-referencing population frequency, independent computational predictors, and existing literature
Possible directions
More genes for a firmer statistical picture; richer structural features beyond distance and confidence
This is a hypothesis-generation tool, not a finished product — the value is in the leads it surfaces.
